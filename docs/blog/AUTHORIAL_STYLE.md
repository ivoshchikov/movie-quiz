# Hard Quiz editorial voice

Established 21 November 2026 (Asia/Nicosia). The owner made an original editorial approach the first priority for every blog article, including revisions of the archive.

## Point of view

Hard Quiz is written by an observant movie enthusiast who helps readers make a decision. Notice a detail, explain what it changes, and connect it to the reader's evening. The distinctive subject is how films work on a viewer: a room that distributes power, a face that contradicts a line, a sound that turns empty space into a threat, or a rule that changes what counts as evidence.

Public articles use natural English. This is a house voice, credited to Hard Quiz, not a fictional human critic or an invented biography. Use “we” for selection decisions and “you” where it helps the reader. Do not slip into an unidentified “I”.

## Build a selection before writing entries

1. Give the article one specific editorial question. A holiday, genre or search phrase supplies a subject; it does not supply a reason for these particular picks.
2. State the inclusion rule and its limits. Explain exceptions. A short, justified selection is preferable to reaching a target number.
3. Compare candidates on a useful axis: kind of attention, source of tension, prior knowledge, emotional weight, viewing company or practical commitment. Supply a reason to prefer one actual title to another.
4. Keep the most useful decision near the beginning. Let the opening have an image, observation or argument of its own; do not start every article with “Start with X for Y”.
5. Check the selection against the question again. If an entry could move unchanged to an unrelated list, its justification needs work.

## Write with a reason to notice

- Keep the premise short. Spend more space on why this film belongs, what distinguishes it from its neighbours, and what might make it a poor fit.
- Anchor interpretation in a specific, supported feature. A familiar detail can produce original reasoning; a decorative adjective cannot.
- A useful observation has a consequence. “The guests share one room” becomes interesting when it explains who can overhear whom, how escape becomes difficult, or why this is a better group watch than the alternative.
- Vary the prose and structure. Do not bolt identical “Watch for”, “Why it matters” or verdict boxes onto every entry. Voice should survive the removal of those labels.
- Use dry, light humour where the material welcomes it. Do not turn real suffering, abuse, illness or historical atrocities into a joke.
- Prefer concrete words and active verbs. Avoid superlatives without criteria, promotional adjectives, generic praise, SEO filler, fake consensus, and formulaic “not X, but Y” constructions.
- Offer precise reservations. Slow pacing, required prior knowledge, graphic material and divided reception are different kinds of limitation; name the relevant one.
- Protect discoveries. An observation about the advertised setup should not spend an ending, a hidden identity, a surprise appearance or a late-film mechanism.

## Evidence and honesty

Facts, third-party opinions and our interpretations are different claims. Link a factual or attributed claim to a source that supports it. Quote sparingly; do not paraphrase a review into a purported personal response. Primary studio, filmmaker, publisher, distributor, technical and service sources take priority for factual details. Attribute criticism when used.

Do not invent having watched, attended, interviewed, measured or tested anything. A source-based recommendation can take a firm position when its reasoning is shown. Evidence of a viewing or first-hand contribution may be added only when it actually exists. Preserve useful AI-assistance and research-method disclosures.

Publication date, substantive editorial modification and factual verification date are separate. Editing the voice does not recheck a streaming catalog. Keep explicit verification dates, territories and edition limits; do not refresh them for appearance. Mark unresolved claims honestly. Future-release previews must distinguish expectations from judgments of a finished film. Archived calendars describe their historical release window.

## Different articles need different forms

| Format | Editorial job | Useful added value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Watchlist | Help choose between real alternatives | An organising question, comparisons, specific appeal and a meaningful reservation |
| “Movies like” | Identify which pleasure of the reference film the reader wants again | Different routes for humour, social observation, puzzle construction or atmosphere; explain distance as well as resemblance |
| Release calendar | Help plan a cinema trip | Reasons to reserve an evening, preparation choices, rollout and format distinctions; no advance-review language |
| Book-to-film guide | Explain what a change of medium means | A sourced structural difference and a practical read-first/watch-first decision |
| Explainer | Give readers a way to see or hear something themselves | A reproducible comparison, a causal explanation and the limits of the demonstration |
| Historical calendar | Preserve a useful record | Period-correct dates and context, with no implication of current availability |

## Before release

Read the complete article aloud in substance: does each paragraph do work? Check the opening, selection rule, comparisons, reservations, factual support, spoiler boundary, current-versus-historical wording and repeated phrases. Preserve URLs, useful sources, image credits, accessibility, external-link behaviour and metadata. Update the substantive modification date and the editorial record. Run the repository's relevant build and publication checks, then verify the released HTML if publication is in scope.

This approach improves editorial quality; it is not a guarantee of AdSense approval or search rankings.
